Adoption at scale

How to scale AI adoption

Tools are rarely the bottleneck. A good use of AI tends to stay with the person who found it. The work is getting it to spread across teams, and stay spread.

Four decisions that decide whether it works

PurposeDecide what champions are for: surfacing real use cases to the central team, building inside their teams, or somewhere between. Write it down and agree it with IT and security.
PeopleSelf-starters their peers already ask, whose managers will give them real time. Not chosen by title, as a reward, or before they know the business.
TrainingOn their own team's work. A champion is ready when they have delivered one real use case.
The day afterWhere most programs stall. Named sponsors, a short briefing for each champion's manager, a network that is easy to join and leave, and learning that doesn't stop.

What a good champion does, and the proof behind each

Asks "should we build this at all?"Reuse, buy, build, or wait, judged on frequency, value, and the cost of a mistake.
Builds the simplest thing that worksA shared prompt, then a skill, then an automation, and an agent only when it is justified.
Writes the spec before the buildWho it is for, what it must never do, and who checks the output.
Owns the sign-offKnows when the output can be trusted, and when it can't.

A small version of this runs in the practice today. In a 25-person practice: a weekly AI enablement segment, in-house usage aids, and tools built for the team's own work. It's a team-level start; doing it across many teams is the next step.

How to measure it

  • Use cases in daily use, not licenses issued
  • Weekly active use, team by team
  • A business number that moved
  • Solutions reused, not rebuilt
  • Champions who still have the time

What to steer away from

  • Chasing the tool of the quarter
  • Training everyone the same way
  • An agent where a prompt would do
  • Champions chosen by title

Why now: people outside engineering are building with AI coding agents. That multiplies both the value and the risk. The answer is enablement and guardrails, not a shutdown, and champions are how that happens safely.

Reference · AI architecture

An approach to AI architecture

AI architecture is the design of an operating capability, not a model choice. Start with the business outcome and the workflow, decide where AI helps and which decisions stay with rules or people, and prove it before scaling.

In 30 seconds

Map the data, permissions, components and integrations, including how each one fails and recovers. Compare the simplest workable pattern with the more complex options and write the tradeoff down. Before anything scales, require evidence on task quality, security, response time, adoption and cost per successful outcome, with a named business owner and a named service owner.

Six domains, one capability

What to look for in each, and the tradeoff to defend.

01 · Outcomes & operating model

Business architecture

  • Map the work before choosing the tool; some problems need rules or a better form, not AI.
  • Score value and feasibility separately, and name an outcome owner with scale, stop and review criteria.
  • Treat adoption as workflow design: roles, training, incentives and support are part of the architecture.

Tradeoff to name: Centralized vs. federated delivery: shared services and guardrails, with accountability in the business domains.

02 · Components & tradeoffs

Solution architecture

  • Keep the model separate from the application, so either can change without rebuilding the other.
  • Use the simplest pattern that works: rules, then a bounded model call, then retrieval, then a workflow, and an agent only when planning really pays.
  • Write decision records: what was chosen, what was rejected, and what would change the call.

Tradeoff to name: Buy vs. extend vs. build, decided by fit, control and total cost, not by novelty.

03 · Knowledge, quality & access

Data architecture

  • Every source has an owner; ingestion runs as a production pipeline, not a one-time load.
  • Enforce access before content reaches the model; the model is never the permission check.
  • Measure retrieval and generation separately, so a bad answer can be traced to its cause.

Tradeoff to name: A copied search index vs. a live query: speed and control against freshness and revocation.

04 · Contracts & reliable actions

Integration architecture

  • Systems of record stay authoritative; AI proposes, trusted code commits.
  • Retries must not create duplicates: stable operation keys and reconciliation when an outcome is unknown.
  • Preserve the user's identity end to end, and scope every tool to the least authority it needs.

Tradeoff to name: A direct API call vs. a queue: immediacy against resilience and recovery.

05 · Trust boundaries & accountability

Security & governance

  • Threat-model the whole system, including prompt injection through retrieved content.
  • Enforce authority outside the model, and test that content can never grant a permission.
  • Make governance an operating rhythm: a fast path for low-risk work, deeper review where the stakes are.

Tradeoff to name: Broad autonomy vs. bounded authority: widen it only as evidence and controls earn it.

06 · Quality, reliability & economics

Production operations

  • Build evaluation into development, and report a balanced scorecard: quality, safety, speed, adoption, cost.
  • Release the whole AI configuration deliberately: prompts, models, retrieval settings and tools are versioned together.
  • Manage cost per useful outcome, not token price.

Tradeoff to name: The cheapest model vs. the cheapest outcome: rework and review time count.

A worked example: intelligent request intake

A design exercise built on , not a claim of a deployed system. It ties the six domains together.

Trusted code System of record Model Owner Operations
  1. Authenticate the caller; trusted code decides what they can see.
  2. Save the original request in the system of record first.
  3. Retrieve only authorized guidance, and only if a simple taxonomy is not enough.
  4. Draft with the model: category, summary, missing fields, all treated as suggestions.
  5. Validate and review: an authorized owner keeps prioritization.
  6. Commit through a narrow adapter with an idempotency key; the model never grants permission.
  7. Measure versions, timing, review edits and accepted outcomes.

If something breaks

  • Model down: intake still works on the manual path.
  • Malicious document: its text cannot authorize an action.
  • Write times out: reconcile by operation key; never create a duplicate.
  • Quality drops: compare versions, switch suggestions off, keep the manual flow.

Claims worth pushing back on

  • “RAG eliminates hallucinations.” It improves access to evidence; retrieval and generation can still fail.
  • “A citation proves the answer.” Only if the passage supports the claim and the source is current and authoritative.
  • “The model says it is 95% confident.” Uncalibrated until it is checked against real errors.
  • “Enterprise licensing makes it compliant.” Compliance depends on the workload, configuration, data use and controls.
  • “We have a human in the loop.” Who reviews what, with which authority, information and workload?
  • “Temperature zero makes it deterministic.” Consistency still depends on the model and the service; test it.
