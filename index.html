Adoption at scale

How I'd help scale AI adoption

Tools are rarely the bottleneck. A good use of AI tends to stay with the person who found it. The work is getting it to spread across teams, and stay spread.

Central AI team
Sets the direction
Platforms, guardrails, priorities, and the hard builds.
guardrails, tools, trainingreal use cases, feedback
Champions in each team
Carry it into the work
They do the team's real work, one step ahead in AI, and move peers without needing authority.
use cases that fit the workwhat works, what's missing
Teams
Use it every day
Measured by changed work, not by logins.

Four decisions that decide whether it works

PurposeDecide what champions are for: surfacing real use cases to the central team, building inside their teams, or somewhere between. Write it down and agree it with IT and security.
PeopleSelf-starters their peers already ask, whose managers will give them real time. Not chosen by title, as a reward, or before they know the business.
TrainingOn their own team's work. A champion is ready when they have delivered one real use case.
The day afterWhere most programs stall. Named sponsors, a short briefing for each champion's manager, a network that is easy to join and leave, and learning that doesn't stop.

What a good champion does, and where I do it

Asks "should we build this at all?"Reuse, buy, build, or wait, judged on frequency, value, and the cost of a mistake.
Builds the simplest thing that worksA shared prompt, then a skill, then an automation, and an agent only when it is justified.
Writes the spec before the buildWho it is for, what it must never do, and who checks the output.
Owns the sign-offKnows when the output can be trusted, and when it can't.

A small version of this runs in my practice today. In a 25-person practice: a weekly AI enablement segment, in-house usage aids, and a few tools built for the team's own work. It's a team-level start; doing it across many teams is the next step.

How I'd measure it

  • Use cases in daily use, not licenses issued
  • Weekly active use, team by team
  • A business number that moved
  • Solutions reused, not rebuilt
  • Champions who still have the time

What I'd steer away from

  • Chasing the tool of the quarter
  • Training everyone the same way
  • An agent where a prompt would do
  • Champions chosen by title

Why now: people outside engineering are building with AI coding agents. That multiplies both the value and the risk. The answer is enablement and guardrails, not a shutdown, and champions are how that happens safely.
